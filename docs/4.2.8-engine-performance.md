# Engine Performance

What the engine's search costs, and where in a game the work falls.

The interface is [4.2.3](4.2.3-engine-interface.md). How performance is
measured is [4.3.10](4.3.10-benchmarking.md), owned by Capacity Planning. In
this page *laptop* is 4.3.10's development machine and *rehearsal* its
rehearsal host. Where the figures below come from:

- the cost model, the floor and the position in a game: three runs a side on
  the laptop and rehearsal, 2026-09-04, commits `ddfb542d`, `1f2b2cec`,
  `ca3e44e0`, `f3a782cd`, `b3468247` and `89f94dbf` (all app 0.7.1). The
  per-move files with `n_moves` are not committed, so the repository cannot
  recount them;
- the machine-to-machine equation: `moves/wide-5x5-c2281dc.csv`, five runs a
  side at commit `c2281dc` (app 0.7.1), which holds no `n_moves`;
- the dictionary and cross-check costs: the CSVs named in that section.

## What a move costs, in terms of the search itself

The machine-to-machine equation in
[4.3.10](4.3.10-benchmarking.md#what-the-machine-difference-actually-is)
relates two machines. This one relates a machine to the work. The variable is
`n_moves` in the per-move file: the candidates `GreedyEngine` iterates, counted
outside the timed region with the same `enumerate_legal_moves` call.

**Cost is a fixed part plus a constant amount per candidate.** Fitted to each
position's fastest run, with the ratios taken from the unrounded fits:

| | fixed (ms) | per candidate (µs) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| laptop | 0.179 | 3.35 |
| rehearsal | 0.286 | 6.97 |
| ratio | **1.59x** | **2.08x** |

**The ratio rises with the work.** It is 1.59x when the search is trivial and
the fixed part dominates, and approaches 2.08x once the per-candidate part
does. The multiplier is constant; the mixture changes.

**The fit is good enough to use, not good enough to call a law.** Fit quality
over all positions:

| fit | R² | R² of log cost | median relative error | mean relative error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| laptop, fixed + linear | 0.9791 | 0.9135 | **12.9%** | 18.1% |
| laptop, + quadratic | 0.9802 | 0.9278 | 11.7% | 16.0% |
| rehearsal, fixed + linear | 0.9732 | 0.8888 | **13.7%** | 23.2% |
| rehearsal, + quadratic | 0.9740 | 0.9065 | 13.2% | 20.6% |

**R² is not the measure to quote here.** The laptop's costs run from 0.09 to
16.4 ms, so plain R² is decided by the largest positions and reads high for
almost any model. R² of the logarithm and the median relative error are the
honest ones: 13%.

**The intercept is not the floor, and the floor can be measured.** Positions
where `n_moves` is zero have nothing to iterate, so what they cost is the fixed
part. There are 147 of them:

| `n_moves` = 0 | laptop (ms) | rehearsal (ms) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| median | **0.124** | 0.177 |
| mean | 0.128 (min 0.091, max 0.202) | not recorded |
| fastest | 0.091 | 0.079 |
| the linear fit's intercept | 0.179 | 0.286 |

Least squares fits the **conditional mean**, so the comparison is against the
mean: the laptop's intercept is 40% above it. **Quote 0.128 ms as the laptop's
fixed cost, not the intercept**, which an intercept fitted across the whole
range pulls up.

**There is a step at the first candidate.** Going from zero candidates to one
costs 28 µs on the laptop; each candidate after that costs 2 to 5 µs. A
position with no legal move is a different shape of work from one with a
single legal move, and the model has one constant for both.

**The machines do not meet at small work.** The fastest single move is 0.091 ms
on the laptop and 0.079 on rehearsal, but across all 147 zero-candidate
positions the medians are 0.124 and 0.177, a ratio of 1.42x (from the
unrounded medians). The equal minima are the extreme of a noisy sample.

**The residuals have shape.** A fitted line predicts the *average* of the
positions sharing a value of `n_moves`, so residuals are measured against each
band's mean, not its median. The 200–800 band has a median of 1.16 ms and a
mean of 1.41; against the median the fit looked 16% out, against the mean it
is 4%. Against the mean the model:

- tracks to within 4% from 10 candidates upward;
- is biased high below that: +9% in the 1–10 band and +40% at zero.

The lack of fit lives at the bottom of the range, where one constant has to
cover both a position with no legal move and one with a few. `n_moves` does
not fully determine cost either: candidates differ in how much work each is
(tiles placed, cross words scored), and the model has no term for that.

**The quadratic is marginally better on every measure and is not used.** Its
coefficient is *negative* on both machines, so it describes saturation, not
growth: the per-candidate cost falls from 35 µs at a handful of candidates to
3.5 µs at three thousand as the fixed part is amortised. It extrapolates to
zero and then negative cost, and buys 1% of accuracy inside the measured
range.

**The machine-to-machine equation fits four times better than either.**
`vm_ms = 2.210 laptop_ms − 0.110` (`vm_ms` is rehearsal's cost) has a median
error of 3.3% where the cost models have 13%. What `n_moves` misses about a
position is a property of the *position*, so both machines pay it and it
cancels in the ratio.

**The two equations agree.** Substituting the laptop model into the
machine-to-machine equation predicts a rehearsal cost of 0.2862 ms + 7.41 µs
per candidate. Fitted directly against `n_moves`, rehearsal gives 0.2859 ms +
6.97 µs: within 0.1% on the fixed part and 6% on the slope.

**`n_moves` understates the work, and that is the model's real limitation.**
It counts what the search *returns*, not what it explored. Enumeration calls
`validate_move` on every placement it makes and keeps the ones that pass
([4.2.1](4.2.1-rules-engine.md#move-enumeration)), so a rejected placement
costs a validation and appears nowhere in `n_moves`. Positions with no legal
move still cost more the more tiles are in the rack:

| rack tiles, no legal move | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| laptop median cost (ms) | 0.098 | 0.109 | 0.110 | 0.124 | 0.126 | **0.145** |

That is half as much again for the same nothing. Adding `blanks` to the model
recovers some of it: median error 12.9% to 10.9%, R² of the logarithm 0.9135 to
0.9432. A blank stands for any letter the cell allows, so it multiplies what
must be tried. A count of rack selections does not help, so the missing
variable is not rack size. **Counting placements tried inside
`enumerate_legal_moves` would measure it; no such counter exists.**

## Where the work is in a game

The endgame is cheap; the opening and middlegame are not. Laptop cost by turn,
where `turn` is the bench's move index within a game and 0 is the opening:

| turn | median candidates | median cost (ms) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 135 | 0.459 |
| **1–3** | **192** | **0.815** |
| 4–7 | 78 | 0.434 |
| 8–11 | 38 | 0.310 |
| 20–25 | 11 | 0.208 |
| 40–49 | 1 | 0.126 |

**The peak is turns 1–3, the second to fourth moves, and each band after it is
cheaper.** Turns 1–3 cost 2.6 times turns 8–11, 3.9 times turns 20–25 and
6.5 times turns 40–49. Turn 0 costs less than them: an empty board has one
anchor, the centre, and enumeration starts every opening move on it
([4.2.1](4.2.1-rules-engine.md#move-enumeration)). Turns 1–3 have an open
board *and* a full rack: the most places to put tiles and the least to stop
you.

**Two causes, and they separate.** The rack empties towards the end of a game,
so a cheap endgame could be the board constraining the search or fewer tiles to
arrange. Holding one and varying the other:

| held | varied | median cost (ms) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rack of 7 | turns 1–3 | 0.815 |
| rack of 7 | turns 8–11 | 0.310 |
| rack of 7 | turns 26–31 | 0.199 |
| rack of 7 | turns 40–59 | 0.155 |
| turn 30 or later | rack of 1 | 0.099 |
| turn 30 or later | rack of 4 | 0.146 |
| turn 30 or later | rack of 7 | 0.195 |

**The board is a five-fold effect and the rack a two-fold one.** With the rack
at seven the cost falls 5.3 to one across a game: tiles landing add
cross-checks that prune the search. From turn 30 the emptying rack adds a
further 2.0 to one, the smaller of the two.

**`n_moves` separates the two ways an engine change can help**: fewer
candidates to consider, or less time spent on each.
