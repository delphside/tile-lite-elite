# Engine performance

The cost of the engine's search and dictionary, and where in a game it falls.

The interface is [4.2.3](4.2.3-engine-interface.md). How performance is
measured is [4.3.10](4.3.10-benchmarking.md). On this page *laptop* is
4.3.10's development machine and *rehearsal* its rehearsal host. Sources:

- the cost model and the position in a game: three runs on each machine,
  2026-09-04, all app 0.7.1. The commits are `ddfb542d` (the fit), `1f2b2cec`
  (goodness of fit), `ca3e44e0` ([the floor](#the-floor)), `f3a782cd`
  (residuals), `b3468247` (rack, blanks and turns) and `89f94dbf` (board

  repository cannot recount them;
- the machine-to-machine equation: the committed
  `crates/server-game/examples/moves/wide-5x5-c2281dc.csv`, five runs on each
  machine at commit `c2281dc` (app 0.7.1). It has `turn`, `blanks` (blank tiles
  in the rack) and `rack_tiles` but no `n_moves`, and is a different run set
  from the first: its medians by turn and by rack run from 7% below to 3% above
  the tables in [Where the work is in a game](#where-the-work-is-in-a-game)
  (turns 1–3: 0.755 ms against 0.815);
- the dictionary and cross-check costs: the CSVs named in that section.

## What a move costs, in terms of the search itself

The machine-to-machine equation in
[4.3.10](4.3.10-benchmarking.md#what-the-machine-difference-actually-is)
relates two machines. This section relates a machine to the work. The variable
is `n_moves` in the per-move file: the candidates `GreedyEngine` iterates,
counted outside the timed region with the same `enumerate_legal_moves` call.
It separates the two ways an engine change can help: fewer candidates to
consider, or less time spent on each.

Cost is an intercept plus a constant amount per candidate (the slope), fitted
to each position's fastest run. The ratios are taken from the unrounded fits:

| Fit | Intercept (ms) | Slope (µs per candidate) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| laptop | 0.179 | 3.35 |
| rehearsal | 0.2859 | 6.97 |
| rehearsal, predicted from the laptop fit by 4.3.10's equation | 0.2862 | 7.41 |

- rehearsal over laptop: 1.59x in the intercepts, 2.08x in the slopes
- the prediction differs from the direct rehearsal fit by 0.1% in the
  intercept and 6% in the slope
- the model's ratio rises with the work, from 1.59x towards 2.08x; measured at
  zero candidates it is 1.42x ([The floor](#the-floor))

Fit quality over all positions, both fits an intercept plus a slope:

| Fit | R² | R² of log cost | Median relative error | Mean relative error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| laptop | 0.9791 | 0.9135 | 12.9% | 18.1% |
| rehearsal | 0.9732 | 0.8888 | 13.7% | 23.2% |

- plain R² is set by the largest positions, so it reads high for almost any
  model; the measures to use are R² of the log cost (0.89 to 0.91) and the
  median relative error (13% to 14%)
- the equation between machines has a median error of 3.3%, about four times
  smaller: what `n_moves` misses about a position belongs to the position, so
  both machines pay it and it cancels in the ratio

### The floor

The *floor* is the cost of a position with no candidate to iterate. The 147
positions where `n_moves` is zero show it:

| `n_moves` = 0 | Laptop (ms) | Rehearsal (ms) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| median | 0.124 | 0.177 |
| mean | 0.128 | not recorded |
| maximum | 0.202 | not recorded |
| fit intercept | 0.179 | 0.2859 |

Rehearsal over laptop, ratio of medians: 1.42x.

A fit predicts the mean ([How the fit errs](#how-the-fit-errs)), so the floor
is compared by its mean: the laptop's intercept is 40% above it, a compromise
across the whole range of costs. Quote the mean, 0.128 ms, as the laptop's
fixed cost; for rehearsal only the median, 0.177 ms, was recorded.

Going from zero candidates to one costs 28 µs on the laptop; each candidate
after that costs 2 to 5 µs. The model has one slope, 3.35 µs, for both.

### How the fit errs

A fitted line predicts the mean of the positions sharing a value of `n_moves`,
so residuals are measured against each band's mean, not its median. Fit
against the band mean:

| Candidates | Fit against the band mean |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | +40% |
| 1–10 | +9% |
| 10 and more | within 4% |

The source states the bands this way and does not say which holds 10.

`n_moves` does not fully determine cost: candidates differ in how much work
each is (tiles placed, cross words scored), and the model has no term for that.

`n_moves` also understates the work. It counts what the search returns, not
what it explored. Enumeration calls `validate_move` on every placement it makes
and keeps the ones that pass
([4.2.1](4.2.1-rules-engine.md#move-enumeration)), so a rejected placement
costs a validation and appears nowhere in `n_moves`. Positions with no legal
move cost more the more tiles are in the rack (no 5-tile figure was recorded):

| Rack tiles, no legal move | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| laptop median cost (ms) | 0.098 | 0.109 | 0.110 | 0.124 | 0.126 | 0.145 |

Seven tiles cost 1.48x one tile. A blank stands for any letter the cell
allows, so it multiplies what must be tried; adding a variable to the laptop
fit:

| Variable added | Median relative error | R² of log cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| none | 12.9% | 0.9135 |
| `blanks` | 10.9% | 0.9432 |
| a count of rack selections | no gain | no gain |

The missing variable is therefore not rack size as such. The placements tried
inside `enumerate_legal_moves` would measure it; nothing counts them.

## Where the work is in a game

Turns 1–3 cost most and the endgame least. Laptop cost for sampled bands of
turns, where `turn` is the bench's move index within a game and 0 is the
opening:

| Turn | Median candidates | Median cost (ms) | Turns 1–3 over this band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 135 | 0.459 | 1.8x |
| 1–3 | 192 | 0.815 | none |
| 4–7 | 78 | 0.434 | 1.9x |
| 8–11 | 38 | 0.310 | 2.6x |
| 20–25 | 11 | 0.208 | 3.9x |
| 40–49 | 1 | 0.126 | 6.5x |

Turn 0 costs less than turns 1–3: an empty board has one anchor, the centre,
and enumeration starts every opening move on it
([4.2.1](4.2.1-rules-engine.md#move-enumeration)). Turns 1–3 have an open board
and a full rack: the most places to put tiles and the fewest constraints.

The rack empties towards the end of a game, so a cheap endgame could be the
board constraining the search or fewer tiles to arrange. Holding one and
varying the other:

| Held | Varied | Median cost (ms) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rack of 7 | turns 1–3 | 0.815 |
| rack of 7 | turns 8–11 | 0.310 |
| rack of 7 | turns 26–31 | 0.199 |
| rack of 7 | turns 40–59 | 0.155 |
| turn 30 or later | rack of 1 | 0.099 |
| turn 30 or later | rack of 4 | 0.146 |
| turn 30 or later | rack of 7 | 0.195 |

- the board: with the rack at seven the cost falls 5.3 to one across a game,
  because tiles landing add cross-checks that prune the search
- the rack: from turn 30 the emptying rack adds a further 2.0 to one

## Design notes on engine performance

- **A quadratic cost model.** Fitted and not used: it improves every measure,
  but only by 0.5 to 1.2 points of median relative error, and its coefficient
  is negative on both machines, so extrapolated it predicts negative cost.
