# Engine Performance

What the engine's search costs, and where in a game the work falls: the
performance its design produces. The interface is [2.3](2.3-engine-interface.md).
How performance is measured, and the benchmark the figures below come from, is
[3.10](3.10-benchmarking.md), owned by Capacity Planning.

## What a move costs, in terms of the search itself

The machine-to-machine equation in [3.10](3.10-benchmarking.md#what-the-machine-difference-actually-is)
relates two machines. This one relates a machine to the work,
and `n_moves` in the per-move file is the variable: the candidates the engine
iterates, counted with the same `enumerate_legal_moves` it uses.

**Cost is a fixed part plus a constant amount per candidate, and nothing more.**
Three runs a side, each position reduced to its minimum:

| | fixed | per candidate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| laptop | 0.179 ms | 3.35 µs |
| rehearsal | 0.286 ms | 6.97 µs |
| ratio | **1.59x** | **2.08x** |

**Which is why the ratio rises with the work.** It is 1.59x when the search is
trivial and the fixed part dominates, and approaches 2.08x once the per-candidate
part does. Nothing about the multiplier changes; the mixture does.

**How well it fits, which is a fair question and the answer is "well enough to
use, not well enough to call a law".**

| fit | R² | R²(log) | median rel. error | mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| laptop, fixed + linear | 0.9791 | 0.9135 | **12.9%** | 18.1% |
| laptop, + quadratic | 0.9802 | 0.9278 | 11.7% | 16.0% |
| rehearsal, fixed + linear | 0.9732 | 0.8888 | **13.7%** | 23.2% |
| rehearsal, + quadratic | 0.9740 | 0.9065 | 13.2% | 20.6% |

**R² is not the measure to quote here.** The costs span three orders of
magnitude, so plain R² is decided by the largest positions and reads high for
almost anything. R² on the logarithm, and the median relative error, are the
honest ones: about 13%.

**The intercept is not the floor, and the floor can be measured instead of
extrapolated.** Positions where `n_moves` is zero have nothing to iterate, so
what they cost *is* the fixed part. There are 147 of them:

| | laptop | rehearsal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N = 0, median | **0.124 ms** | 0.177 ms |
| N = 0, fastest | 0.091 ms | 0.079 ms |
| what the linear fit predicts | 0.179 ms (OLS), 0.145 (weighted) | 0.286 ms |

The fit over-predicts the floor. Least squares fits the **conditional mean**,
so the comparison has to be against the mean of those 147 positions, which is
0.128 ms — they are a tight group, min 0.091 and max 0.202, so the mean and the
median nearly agree. The fit is 40% above it either way. **Quote 0.128 ms as the
fixed cost, not the intercept**: an intercept fitted across three orders of
magnitude is a compromise pulled up by the rest of the range.

**There is also a step at the first candidate.** Going from zero candidates to
one costs about 28 µs on the laptop; each candidate after that costs 2 to 5 µs.
A position with no legal move is a different shape of work from one with a
single legal move, and the model has one constant for both.

**Which also retires an earlier reading.** The fastest single move being equal
on the two machines — 0.091 ms against 0.079 — looked like the machines meeting
at small work. Across all 147 zero-candidate positions the medians are 0.124 and
0.177, a ratio of **1.42x**. The crossing was the extreme of a noisy sample, not
a plateau, and the population says the VM is slower even here.

**And the residuals have shape.** A fitted line predicts the *average* of the positions
sharing a value of `n_moves`, so residuals must be measured against the mean of
each band and not its median. At large N those differ a lot — the 200–800 band
has a median of 1.16 ms and a mean of 1.41 — and comparing against the median
made the fit look 16% out where against the mean it is 4%.

Measured properly, the model tracks the conditional mean to within about 4%
from 10 candidates upward, and is biased **high below that**: +9% in the 1–10
band and +40% at zero. So the lack of fit is real but lives at the bottom of the
range, where the fixed cost dominates and one constant has to cover both a
position with no legal move and one with a few.

`n_moves` still does not fully determine cost — candidates differ in how much
work each is, in word length and cross-checks — and the model has no term for
that. That is what the remaining spread within each band is.

**The quadratic is marginally better on every measure and still should not be
used.** Its coefficient is *negative* on both machines: it is not describing
growth but saturation, the per-candidate cost falling from 35 µs at a handful of
candidates to 3.5 µs at three thousand as the fixed part is amortised. A
negative quadratic extrapolates to zero and then negative cost, so it buys 1%
of accuracy inside the measured range at the price of being nonsense outside it.

**The machine-to-machine equation fits four times better than either of these,
and that is informative rather than odd.** `vm_ms = 2.210 laptop_ms − 0.110`
sits at 3.3% median error where the cost models sit at 13%. Whatever `n_moves`
fails to capture about a position — how long its words are, how many cross-checks
each candidate needs — is a property of the *position*, so both machines pay it
and it cancels in the ratio. Comparing two machines is an easier question than
predicting a cost from first principles, and the numbers say so.

**The two equations agree, which is the check that matters.** Substituting the
laptop model into `vm_ms = 2.210 laptop_ms − 0.110` predicts a VM cost of
`0.2862 ms + 7.41 µs` per candidate. Fitted directly against `n_moves` the VM
gives `0.2859 ms + 6.97 µs`. Two independent routes, agreeing to four decimals
on the fixed part and 6% on the slope.

**`n_moves` understates the work, and that is the model's real limitation**
It counts what the search *returns*, not what it explored
to get there — every rejected placement costs something and appears nowhere in
it. The proof is the zero-output positions, which have no legal move at all and
still cost more the more tiles are in the rack:

| rack tiles, with **no legal move** | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| median cost | 0.098 | 0.109 | 0.110 | 0.124 | 0.126 | **0.145 ms** |

Half again as much work for the same nothing. Adding `blanks` to the model
recovers some of this — 12.9% to 10.9% median error, R²(log) 0.9135 to 0.9432 —
because a blank multiplies what must be tried by the letters it could stand for.
A crude count of rack selections does not help at all, so the missing variable is
not simply rack size. **Measuring it properly means counting inside
`enumerate_legal_moves` — placements tried rather than placements returned —
which is a change to a shipping crate and has not been made.**

## Where the work is in a game

The endgame is cheap; the opening and middlegame are not.

| turn | median candidates | median cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 135 | 0.459 ms |
| **1–3** | **192** | **0.815 ms** |
| 4–7 | 78 | 0.434 ms |
| 8–11 | 38 | 0.310 ms |
| 20–25 | 11 | 0.208 ms |
| 40–49 | 1 | 0.126 ms |

**The peak is the third move, and then it falls monotonically.** Turn 0 is
cheaper than what follows because an empty board has one anchor. Turns 1–3 have
an open board *and* a full rack: the most places to put tiles and the least to
stop you.

**Two causes, and they separate cleanly.** The rack empties towards the end of a
game, so a cheap endgame could be the board constraining the search or simply
fewer tiles to arrange. Holding one and varying the other answers it:

| rack held at 7 | | turn held at 30 or later | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| turns 1–3 | 0.815 ms | rack 1 | 0.099 ms |
| turns 8–11 | 0.310 ms | rack 4 | 0.146 ms |
| turns 26–31 | 0.199 ms | rack 7 | 0.195 ms |
| turns 40–59 | 0.155 ms | | |

**The board is a five-fold effect and the rack a two-fold one.** With the rack
never leaving seven tiles the cost still falls five to one across a game, so
most of the decline is cross-checks pruning the search as tiles land. The rack
adds a further doubling at the very end, once it starts emptying — real, and the
smaller of the two.

**Which is where to look for a faster bot.** The opening is 4 to 6 times the
cost of the middlegame and 7 times the endgame, and it is the part a player
waits through first.

**So an engine change can be read two ways**, and `n_moves` is what separates
them: fewer candidates to consider, or less time spent on each.
