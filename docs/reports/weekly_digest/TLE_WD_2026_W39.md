### From the issues closed this week

#### #214 MAIN PROJECT: One deployment path, three environments, one build

**R1 was met as written; R2 was met in substance and not in form, and the difference is the lesson.** R2 said *"one tooling across every environment"* and the design that followed was *a thin wrapper, rehearsal's shape — `exec deploy.sh`*. That cannot work, and the reason was in `deploy.sh` the whole time: its delivery phase calls `require_ssh_access`, then `scp` to `$REMOTE`, then several `ssh` blocks, and `REMOTE="$DEPLOY_USER@$DEPLOY_HOST"` is unconditional. Preview must run with no network at all, so the wrapper would have tried to ssh to production's default host.

**A design can be settled and still be untried.** This one survived from 2026-08-24 to 2026-09-20 because nobody had attempted the first line of it — the gap was found by a session that tried to implement and could not, which is a cheaper way to find it than a session that implements and ships.

**What the requirement was actually for, and what was delivered.** R2's purpose was that preview stop being a second implementation that has already diverged. That is met: preview now loads the same `artifacts/<sha>.tar.gz` and its images are *the same image ids* as the ones rehearsal and production load — `5873e9f21a9a` and `db0e418d7a2d`. The **build** is shared; the **deploy** is not, because preview's is inherently local and has nothing to delegate to.

**So a requirement's wording and its purpose can come apart**, and the check written from the wording then asks for the wrong thing. #355's R2 check — *"one script serves all three environments"* — is left unticked rather than reworded, so the divergence between what was asked and what was needed stays visible to whoever reads it next.

**And the gain was larger than the requirement claimed.** Nobody wrote down that a full lap built the same commit twice. Sharing the artefact made the second deploy of a commit take seconds instead of three minutes, which is not in R1 or R2 and is the thing that will be noticed daily.

#### #328 Scripts report to a terminal only, so a failure that nobody was watching leaves prose in a scrollback

**The tests earned their place twice, and both faults were silent ones.** Bash
keeps one EXIT trap, and `deploy.sh` sets its own 1,300 lines after it sources
the library — so an unchained trap would have dropped the outcome of every
deploy, which is the single run this exists for. Then, in a chained trap, `$?`
is the previous command's status, so `cleanup; run_log_finish` recorded whatever
the cleanup returned. Neither would have produced an error: the file would have
existed, looked reasonable, and been wrong.

**A checker that reads code must know a comment from a call.** The declaration
check counted the library's own usage example and reported an event nothing
writes — and that example named `gate` where the real event is `gate passed`, so
the first thing the check found was an inaccuracy in the thing it checks.

**The location was decided by a fact a week old.** A path inside the repository
was the obvious answer when this was raised, and the two worktrees created on
2026-09-17 made it wrong: `main`'s record and `projectdev`'s would have been in
different directories, so *"has this failed before"* would depend on where you
were standing. A design settled before a change of ground needs re-asking after
it, and nothing prompts that.

**I got the paperwork wrong and the board caught it.** I wrote *no milestone, no
delivery-log row*, reasoning from `scripts/` never reaching the image — which is
the rule for a **pre-approved change**, not for a delivery of a project.
`board-check.py` refused it at `Post-deployment`. The check I had spent the day
extending caught its author, which is the only real test of one.

**And a check nearly reported as passed had proved nothing.** R5 was run from
both worktrees and produced one file, which reads as success. `projectdev` was on
an older commit without the wiring and had written nothing at all. The second run,
after syncing, is the one that counts — and the issue says so rather than showing
only the result that looked right.

#### #398 #329 WP A Del 1 of 2: the errors documented, the gate, and the scripts' refusals

**A check piped through `sed` reports `sed`.** `check-docs.sh` ran section 5 as
`check-api-errors.py | sed ... | sed ...` to indent its output, so the section
could have been made a gate at any point and would still never have failed.
Sections 6 and 7 run bare, with a comment saying why — the knowledge was in the
file and one section did not have it. This is the `PIPESTATUS` hazard from
`feedback_verify_by_exit_status` living *inside* a script rather than around one,
and it is invisible while the check passes, which is most of the time.

**A title that states a measurement goes stale silently.** This issue was called
*"The API can return 44 distinct errors and documents none of them"* for two
weeks after they were all documented, and it made the board read worse than the
work. A title naming the subject — what should be true — survives the work being
done; a title naming a count does not.

**A check becomes a gate when its reason for being a check expires, and nothing
notices.** `check-api-errors.py` was report-only for a good reason: a build must
not go red over writing that has not happened yet. The debt reached nil some time
ago and the check stayed a check, because nothing asks *is this still true* of a
decision made once. Worth watching for elsewhere — the same shape applies to any
tolerance granted while a backlog is worked off.

**Scope by measuring the thing, not the population.** R3 looked like 38 scripts
without `--help`. Counting refusal paths instead turned it into eight that matter,
and the twelve with one or two were left deliberately. The population was the
wrong denominator, and the right one was cheap to compute.

#### #403 #291 WP A Del 1 of 5: the capacity plan, its charts and its recurrence

**A report needed vocabulary this project didn't have, and the vocabulary
turned out to be industry-standard rather than something to invent.** Peak,
spike and interval had no agreed meaning here until the owner supplied them —
a spike is a rate with a duration, not a count in a window; the peak's
interval is derived from the workload's own statistics, not chosen by
convention. Both corrected an earlier draft of the design note rather than
extending it cleanly, which is the shape to watch for: getting the framing
wrong is cheaper to fix early than after it has spread into three other
issues.

**A resting sample is not a measurement, and the report said so honestly
rather than padding the gap.** Every figure in the first report was a single
instantaneous reading; the forecast table said *not enough data* in every row
rather than fitting a line through one point. That produced no findings, and
turned out to be the right amount of humility — the alternative was a chart
that looked confident and meant nothing.

**Two real omissions were caught by the owner reading the report, not by any
check.** CPU dropped out of every section after the first table because it
never made it into `measurements.csv` — a plain oversight, invisible to any
tooling since nothing asserts the first table and the CSV agree. And the
ceiling itself turned out to be softer than stated: production is a VM on
OCI, and `engine_timing_results.csv`'s existing `steal_pct_of_capacity`
column already measured the hypervisor taking 2.6% to 4.4% of the machine —
data that existed and was not connected to the capacity plan until asked
about directly.

**What this suggests for #407's register**: a report with generated sections
(the charts, the history table) and hand-written ones (the narrative) has a
seam where the hand-written half can silently omit something the generated
half would have caught automatically if it existed. Worth a check, someday,
that every metric in `measurements.csv` appears in the narrative and vice
versa — not raised as an issue now, recorded here so it is not lost.

**Delegated nothing to the parent.** This delivery's lessons are its own; the
parent's design note (#291) went on to a different, larger correction — R5
was overclaimed as satisfied by RET-3 — which is its own lesson, on #408, not
this one's.
